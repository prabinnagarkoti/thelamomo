Introduction    
The contemporary hospitality industry is increasingly defined by digital dependency. In the post-pandemic landscape, online ordering and delivery have transitioned from secondary revenue streams to operational necessities for restaurants worldwide. However, this digital shift has led to the rise of third-party aggregator platforms, such as Uber Eats and DoorDash, which have fundamentally altered restaurant economics. While these platforms offer visibility and convenience, they extract exorbitant commission fees—often ranging between 20% and 35% per transaction. For large hospitality groups, these fees are a cost of doing business; for independent, micro-hospitality operators running on razor-thin profit margins, these fees represent an unsustainable erosion of revenue. Furthermore, these platforms monopolize the customer relationship, retaining all transaction data and preventing restaurants from building independent brand loyalty. Consequently, small food vendors are trapped in a cycle of dependency: they cannot afford to leave the platforms, but they struggle to remain profitable while using them. It is within this challenging economic environment that the need for independent, commission-free digital infrastructure becomes critical.               
                                                                                       
Section 1: Project Background
1.1 Problem Background
Thela Momo is a small Nepali food cart on Handford Road, Zillmere in Brisbane's northern suburbs. It has a 4.6-star Google rating and a solid local following. Despite doing well, it faces the same problem as thousands of other small food businesses in Australia — the digital tools available to them are either too expensive, too technical, or simply not built with small operators in mind.
Third-party platforms like Uber Eats take between 20 and 35 percent of every sale (Chan et al., 2023). For a food cart running on net margins of 3 to 9 percent, that leaves almost nothing. What makes it worse is that the customer data from every order — who bought what and when — belongs to the platform, not the restaurant (Fu et al., 2024). The business has no way to contact its own customers or build any kind of loyalty outside the platform. Huang and Siao (2023) point out that online food delivery has actually created new forms of dependency for the businesses it was supposed to help.
The alternative is a white-label platform. But these come with their own problems. GloriaFood charges up to $97 per month for features that should come standard. ChowNow starts at $229 per month. Square — which Thela Momo currently uses — is a general payment tool, not a food ordering system. It has no food images, no delivery map, no order tracking, and no way for customers to contact the restaurant after placing an order.
There is no affordable, commission-free, easy-to-use ordering system built for small Australian food businesses. BizMenu Builder was built to fill that gap.
1.2 Project Objectives
BizMenu Builder was developed as a commission-free digital ordering platform for small food businesses. The project ran over twelve weeks with the goal of delivering a working system by Week 12. Six objectives guided the work:
To provide commission-free ordering with no platform fees on any transaction
To give restaurant owners a configurable branded storefront they can manage without technical knowledge
To allow owners to add, update, and manage menu items through an admin dashboard
To implement secure login with separate access levels for owners and customers
To give customers a way to track their order and communicate directly with the restaurant
To deploy the system on cloud infrastructure at no ongoing cost

Section 2: Literature Review
2.1 Platform-by-Platform Analysis
The global online food delivery market was worth USD $52.6 billion in 2024 and is expected to reach USD $93.3 billion by 2030 (Inventco Labs, 2025). To understand what already exists and what is missing, we looked at five platforms — Uber Eats and DoorDash as aggregator marketplaces, and GloriaFood, ChowNow, and Square as white-label alternatives. Square is the platform Thela Momo currently uses, which makes it the most directly relevant comparison. Fu et al. (2024) note that the spread of these platforms has created real problems for the small businesses that depend on them.
Uber Eats
Uber Eats operates in over 12,000 cities and has more than 88 million active users globally (Deliverect, 2025). Figure 2.1 is taken directly from their own fee documentation, updated March 2026. The commission tiers are: Lite 20%, Plus 25–30%, and Premium 30% for delivery. Pickup is charged at 7–10%. For a business like Thela Momo — average orders of $20–40, net margins of 3–9% — even the cheapest plan makes most delivery orders unprofitable. The restaurant also has no access to the customer data generated through the platform. That all stays with Uber Eats.
Figure 2.1 — Uber Eats Marketplace Fee Changes (Source: help.uber.com, 2026).
DoorDash
DoorDash works similarly. The standard plan charges 30% on delivery and 15% on pickup — including in-store collection where DoorDash is not involved at all. The Self-Delivery option is 15% plus a flat AU$9.99 per order. Like Uber Eats, DoorDash keeps all customer data and controls the full ordering experience. Restaurants using DoorDash have no way to contact their own customers independently (Dai & Wu, 2023).
Figure 2.2 — DoorDash for Merchants Pricing (Source: merchants.doordash.com, 2026). 
GloriaFood (Oracle)
GloriaFood is often promoted as a free alternative to aggregators. The base ordering widget genuinely is free, but Figure 2.3 shows what happens when you look at the full picture. The POS system costs $49/month, online payment processing costs $29/month, and marketing tools cost $19/month. A restaurant that needs all three — which most would — is looking at $97/month minimum. After being acquired by Oracle, the platform's support quality has also declined (Menubly, 2026). GloriaFood also has no order tracking page for customers and no direct messaging between customer and restaurant.
Figure 2.3 — GloriaFood Optional Paid Services — Essential Features Are Paywalled (Source: gloriafood.com, 2026). 
ChowNow
ChowNow is commission-free and allows branded storefronts. The problem is the price. As Figure 2.4 shows, the cheapest plan starts at $229/month. For a larger restaurant doing high volumes that might be reasonable, but for a small food cart like Thela Momo it is a lot to commit to before receiving a single order. ChowNow also requires its own hardware device to receive orders.
Figure 2.4 — ChowNow Pricing Plans (Source: chownow.com, 2026). 
Square — Thela Momo's Current Platform
Square is what Thela Momo uses right now, which makes it the closest comparison to BizMenu Builder. Square's fees are lower than the aggregators — around 1.6–1.9% per transaction in Australia (Square Australia, 2025) — and there is no monthly subscription. But looking at Thela Momo's actual Square ordering page in Figure 2.5, the limitations are obvious. There are no food photos on the menu. There is no map for delivery addresses. Once an order is placed, the customer gets no tracking page — they have no idea what is happening with their food. And there is no way to contact the restaurant directly. The storefront sits on Square's generic subdomain rather than Thela Momo's own domain. Square was built as a payments tool, not a restaurant ordering system, and that shows.
Figure 2.5 — Thela Momo's Current Online Ordering System — Square Platform (2026). 
The key issue across all five platforms is business independence. On every aggregator, the restaurant is entirely dependent on the platform — it cannot reach its own customers, cannot track its own data, and loses a significant cut of every sale. The white-label alternatives fix the commission problem but either cost too much (ChowNow), hide fees behind a free tier (GloriaFood), or lack basic features that a food ordering system should have (Square). BizMenu Builder was built specifically to address all of these gaps for a business like Thela Momo.

2.3 Gap Analysis
Looking across all five platforms, three gaps stand out that none of them address.
The first is cost. There is no free, commission-free ordering system that actually has all the features a small restaurant needs. GloriaFood locks payment processing and other essentials behind paid tiers that add up to at least $78–$97 per month. ChowNow starts at $229 per month. Square has no monthly fee but is missing too many things to be useful as a food ordering system. Ocloo et al. (2024) found that owner technical skill is the biggest factor in whether small restaurants can adopt digital tools — so any workable solution also needs to be simple enough to run without IT knowledge. BizMenu Builder runs on open-source technology with no commission and no monthly cost.
The second gap is communication. None of the five platforms give customers a direct way to contact the restaurant after placing an order. Square and GloriaFood have nothing at all once the order is submitted. Uber Eats and DoorDash handle any contact through their own support systems, which means the restaurant never speaks directly to the customer. BizMenu Builder includes a message thread inside each order record, so the customer and the restaurant owner can communicate directly without going through a third party.
The third gap is data ownership. Every platform reviewed stores customer and order data in its own shared system. The restaurant has no independent access to that data. This means no loyalty programmes, no direct marketing, and no real picture of who their customers are (Sharffudin & Yadav, 2025). BizMenu Builder gives each restaurant its own isolated database. Every order and every customer record belongs to the business, not to a platform.

Section 3: Design
3.1 System Architecture
BizMenu Builder implements a four-layer full-stack JavaScript architecture: Users Layer (Customer + Restaurant Owner via HTTPS) → Frontend Layer (Next.js App Router + React Context on Vercel) → Backend Layer (Next.js API Routes as serverless functions: /api/menu, /api/orders, /api/auth) → Database Layer (MongoDB Atlas with four collections: Users, MenuItems, Orders, RestaurantConfig). The Mongoose ODM is used between the API routes and database for validation. The team had changed from Render to Vercel in Sprint 1 for its better support for Next.js CI/CD (Lehmann & Recker, 2022).
3.2 Database Model
The system uses four MongoDB collections. The Users collection stores each user's email, a bcrypt-hashed password, their role (either 'owner' or 'customer'), and their name. The role field is what controls access throughout the whole application — it determines what each user can and cannot do. MenuItems holds the name, description, price, category, and an availability flag called isAvailable. This defaults to true, so any item added through the Menu Manager appears on the customer storefront straight away. The Orders collection stores the customer's contact details, the list of items ordered, the order total, the current status, and a messages array. The messages are stored as embedded subdocuments inside the order record rather than a separate collection, because they are always needed alongside the order itself. Finally, RestaurantConfig holds a single document containing all the branding settings — name, tagline, logo URL, colours, badges, and contact information. It uses upsert:true on save so there is never more than one config document per deployment.

4.3 Cart Side-Sheet
User Story P-04: As a customer, I want to add items to my cart and adjust quantities so I can manage my order before checking out.
Acceptance Criteria:
Clicking +Add opens the cart side-sheet with the item at quantity 1
Each item has a − button to decrease and a + button to increase quantity
Reducing quantity to zero removes the item from the cart
The order total updates correctly every time a quantity changes
Cart contents persist when navigating between pages
Implementation:
The cart is managed through React Context so the contents stay the same even if the customer goes back to browse more items. Each item in the cart shows the name, unit price, quantity controls, and the line total for that item. The running total is calculated inside CartContext as the sum of price multiplied by quantity for every item. It recalculates on every change without any server call. When the customer moves to checkout, the full cart — items and calculated total — is included in the order payload sent to /api/orders. The server then checks the total against the current database prices before saving the order.

4.5 Automated Transactional Emails
User Story S-01 (partial): As a customer, I want to receive a verification email when I register and a confirmation email when I place an order.
Acceptance Criteria:
A 6-digit OTP is sent to the customer's email immediately on registration
The OTP is generated using a secure random function
An order confirmation email with an itemised receipt is sent automatically on order placement
Neither email requires manual action from the restaurant owner
Implementation:
BizMenu Builder sends two types of automated email via Nodemailer and Gmail SMTP. The first is the registration verification email. When a customer registers, a 6-digit OTP is generated and emailed straight away. The customer must enter this code before their account is activated. The second is the order confirmation, sent automatically the moment an order is placed — no owner approval needed. This is safe because the owner controls which items are visible on the menu. If something is unavailable, they mark it off in the Menu Manager and it disappears from the storefront immediately. Any order that gets submitted only contains items that were available at that moment.

4.6 Admin Dashboard Overview
User Story K-04: As a restaurant owner, I want a dashboard overview so I can quickly see how the business is doing without having to dig through individual orders.
Acceptance Criteria:
Dashboard shows four KPI cards: Active Orders, Delivered, Cancelled, and Revenue

Implementation:
The dashboard is protected by two checks. On the client side, a useEffect hook checks the session role and redirects anyone who is not an owner before the page loads. On the server side, every admin API route runs getServerSession and rejects requests from non-owner sessions. The four KPI cards each run a separate MongoDB query on page load — Active Orders counts documents with status 'In Process', Delivered and Cancelled do the same for their statuses, and Revenue runs an aggregation that sums the total field across all Delivered orders.

4.8 Admin Menu Manager
User Story K-02: As a restaurant owner, I want to add and manage menu items so customers always see what is currently available.

Add New Item form collects name, price, description, and a dish photo
New items appear on the customer storefront immediately after saving

Implementation:
The Menu Manager has an inline form at the top for adding items. When submitted, it POSTs to /api/menu, which checks the owner session before calling MenuItem.create(). Implementation:
The Settings page covers all the branding fields stored in the RestaurantConfig document. When the owner saves, a PATCH goes to /api/config which runs findOneAndUpdate with upsert:true. This addresses the main branding limitation we identified with Thela Momo's Square page — where the storefront was stuck on Square's generic subdomain with no real customisation options.

Figure 7.1 — lib/mongodb.ts — Serverless DB Singleton. This code establishes a highly efficient MongoDB connection optimized for Next.js serverless environments. It utilizes a global caching mechanism to prevent connection spiking during hot-reloads and executes mongoose.connect() without buffering commands, ensuring the connection promise is securely cached across all serverless API invocations.
Figure 7.2 — models/MenuItem.ts — Mongoose Schema. This Mongoose schema defines the strict structural requirements for menu items. It enforces strict data types, such as a Number requirement for pricing, and utilizes a default Boolean available field to seamlessly manage storefront visibility toggling. The inclusion of timestamps: true automatically manages document creation and update records.
Figure 7.3 — app/api/menu/route.ts — Full CRUD API Route. This file demonstrates a complete RESTful implementation. While the GET handler remains public to serve the storefront, the POST, PATCH, and DELETE operations are strictly protected. They utilize NextAuth's getServerSession to validate the owner role before executing Mongoose methods like MenuItem.create() or findByIdAndUpdate().
Figure 7.4 — lib/email.ts — Nodemailer Email Service. This utility securely configures a Nodemailer transport instance using Gmail's SMTP servers and environment variables. It abstracts the logic for two critical automated communications: dispatching a randomized 6-digit OTP during registration, and dynamically injecting an itemised receipt array into an HTML template upon order confirmation.
Figure 7.5 — app/dashboard/layout.tsx — Server-Side Role Guard. This asynchronous Server Component acts as a strict security barrier for the admin dashboard. By utilizing getServerSession to retrieve the JWT payload and validating the owner role before the page renders, it can execute a server-side redirect to the login page, completely preventing unauthorized client-side code exposure.
Section 8: Testing & Quality Assurance
Testing was done as structured functional testing mapped to the acceptance criteria of each user story. Tests were run on Chrome, Firefox, and Safari across desktop and mobile devices. Each test area was executed by the team member who built that part of the system (Ahmad et al., 2024). A total of 28 test cases were run across four areas — authentication, customer ordering, order tracking and messaging, and admin management. 25 passed and 3 failed, giving an overall pass rate of 89%.
Authentication testing covered registration, login, and route protection. All nine test cases passed. Sandip Pathak executed this area. Tests confirmed that accounts are created correctly with bcrypt-hashed passwords, that login sets the session role, and that unauthenticated users are redirected before the dashboard loads.
The customer ordering flow was tested by Prabin Nagarkoti across nine test cases, of which seven passed and two failed. The passing tests confirmed that the menu displays correctly, the cart opens with the right item and price, quantity controls update the total, checkout saves the order to MongoDB, and the confirmation email arrives immediately. The two failures were T-11b — the Popular Right Now panel shows a static placeholder instead of querying by order frequency — and T-15b — a slight misalignment on the cart checkout button on Safari mobile at 375px viewport width. These are tracked as Defects D-01 and D-02.
Order tracking and messaging was also tested by Sandip. All four test cases passed. The order tracking page correctly reflects status changes made by the owner, and the message thread between customer and restaurant works as expected.
Admin dashboard and management was tested by Kulwinder Kaur across six test cases. Five passed and one failed. The passing tests confirmed that KPI cards show live counts, order status updates work, menu items can be added and toggled, and settings changes appear on the storefront immediately. The failure was T-25b — the edit menu item modal does not pre-populate with existing values when the owner clicks edit. This is tracked as Defect D-03.
Three defects were identified in total. D-01 is the static Popular Right Now panel, which requires a MongoDB aggregation query and is planned as a future enhancement. D-02 is the Safari mobile cart misalignment, which needs a Tailwind responsive fix. D-03 is the edit modal pre-population issue, caused by a useEffect dependency array bug that is still being worked on. All three defects are linked to the test cases that found them — T-11b, T-15b, and T-25b respectively.
Section 9: Conclusions
9.1 Summary of Project Achievements
BizMenu Builder started as a set of Figma wireframes and ended up as a working, deployed web application — built and delivered within twelve weeks. Most of what we set out to do was completed.
The core ordering flow works end to end. Customers can browse the menu, add items to the cart, go through checkout with the Leaflet map, and receive an automatic confirmation email the moment the order is placed. The order is saved to MongoDB with no commission taken. The restaurant owner gets a full admin dashboard with live order counts, a menu manager, and a settings page that lets them update the storefront branding without touching any code.
Two things are not fully complete. The menu manager works for adding, hiding, and deleting items, but the edit modal does not pre-populate with existing values when the owner tries to edit an item. The API for editing works correctly — the issue is a bug in the frontend useEffect that we have not had time to fix. That is tracked as Defect D-03. The Popular Right Now panel on the homepage is also still a static placeholder. Making it pull from real order data requires a MongoDB aggregation query, which we deprioritised to focus on higher priority features. That is Defect D-01.
Everything else from the original six objectives was delivered. The storefront reads from RestaurantConfig so the owner can change the name, tagline, and branding from the Settings page and it updates immediately. Authentication uses bcrypt for password hashing and NextAuth for session management, with role-based access checked on both the client and server side. Customers can track their order at /order/[id] and send messages directly to the restaurant. The whole system runs on Vercel and MongoDB Atlas at no ongoing cost.
9.2 Lessons Learned
Deployment is a continuous discipline, not a final-stage task. Every sprint produced a deployment issue. Treating environment variables, import paths, and CI/CD configuration as first-class concerns from Sprint 1 — not afterthoughts — was the key lesson.
Open-source tools remove billing risk. Replacing Google Maps with Leaflet/OpenStreetMap eliminated API billing concerns entirely while providing equivalent functionality — a decision that should be made earlier in future projects.
Data ownership must be architectural, not retrospective. Designing with an isolated MongoDB Atlas cluster from the outset — per Ocloo et al. (2024) — was a deliberate decision that was never revisited. Owner control over digital infrastructure must be built in from the start.
Section 10: Future Improvements
There are several things we would like to add to BizMenu Builder in future development cycles.
The most important is online payment. Right now the system only supports Cash on Delivery, which is fine for a prototype but would need to change before the platform could be used in a real production setting. Integrating a payment gateway like Stripe is the top priority.
Two known defects also need to be resolved. The Popular Right Now panel on the homepage is currently a static placeholder — it needs a MongoDB aggregation query to pull the most frequently ordered items from the Orders collection. The menu item edit modal also needs to be fixed so it pre-populates with existing values when the owner clicks edit. The API works, the issue is on the frontend side.
The dashboard currently requires a manual page refresh to show new orders. Adding a notification system using WebSockets or a tool like Pusher would mean the owner gets an alert straight away when a new order comes in, without having to keep refreshing.
On the menu management side, images currently need to be entered as a URL rather than uploaded directly. Adding direct image upload through a service like Cloudinary would make the process much simpler for owners who are not technically confident.
A few lower priority ideas are also worth considering for later. A customer loyalty programme would be straightforward to build since BizMenu Builder already owns all the order data — something that is not possible on aggregator platforms. A basic analytics view showing things like average order value or peak ordering times could also be useful for owners. Further down the line, adding Progressive Web App support would let customers install the storefront on their phone's home screen, and the system could eventually be extended to support multiple restaurants as a small SaaS platform.
